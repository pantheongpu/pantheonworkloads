The harbour woke before the town did. Gulls walked the wet planks, and the first boat
slid out past the lamp on the breakwater with its engine muttering. A girl sat on an
upturned crate and counted the nets: seven blue, three green, one red that nobody owned.
By noon the wind had turned. The flags on the customs house stood out stiff and square,
and the old men on the bench said rain would come by evening, as they said every day.
Sometimes they were right. Today the clouds gathered over the hills, grey on grey, and
the first drops struck the tin roof of the fish shed like a handful of thrown coins.
Inside, the smell of salt and tar and cold iron. A radio played a song with no words.
Somebody laughed at the far end, then stopped, as if the laugh had been borrowed.
When the rain eased the girl walked up the hill road, past the bakery and the chapel,
past the shop that sold rope and candles and postcards of a town that looked nicer.
At the top she turned and looked back: the harbour, the breakwater, the small bright boats
coming home one by one, each with a lantern lit, each trailing a long yellow line
across the dark water. She counted them twice. Eleven out, eleven home. She went in to supper.
